### 1st declension

- A few 1st declension nouns are masculine, particularly jobs: agricola, -ae (farmer); nauta, -ae (sailor); poeta, -ae (poet).
- dea and filia have irregular dative and ablative plurals to distinguish them from the masculine:
    - dea → deabus (deus → deis)
    - filia → filiabus (filius → filiis)

### 2nd declension

- deus is often contracted:
    - dei → di (nom. pl.)
    - deorum → deum (gen. pl.)
    - deis → dis (dat./abl. pl.)
- Some have irregular nominatives: vir, viri (man); puer, pueri (boy); ager, agri (field); liber, libri (book).

### 3rd declension

- The genitive plural is sometimes -ium instead of -um. This happens when the genitive singular has the same number of syllables as the nominative singular: navis, navis → navium (ship).
- Monosyllabic nouns whose stem ends in two consonants also take -ium: urbs, urbis → urbium (city).
- Some nouns don’t (namely family members and dog):
    - canis, canis → canum (dog)
    - frater, fratris → fratrum (brother); mater and pater are the same
    - iuvenis, iuvenis → iuvenum (young man)
    - senex, senis → senum (old man)
- mare (n) is irregular: the ablative singular is mari, and the nom./acc. plural is maria.
- Iuppiter has the stem Iov-.
- Occasionally, nave doesn’t need a preposition.

### 4th declension

- domus (house) has the ablative singular domo, the accusative plural domos, and a locative domi.
- The accusative domum is used without a preposition for home/homewards.

### 5th declension

- dies (day) is usually masculine, but can be feminine in the singular if it refers to a special day.

### Uses of the cases

#### Nominative

- Subject of the verb
- Complement, often after a part of sum
- Apposition (nouns can be in apposition in other cases too)
- In indirect statement, as the subject (before the ATN verb) where the ATN verb is impersonal

#### Accusative

- Direct object of the verb
- After certain prepositions (usually motion towards)
- Indirect statement, with an ATN verb and an infinitive
- Accusative of respect (usually body parts)
- Time how long
- Exclamations

#### Genitive

- Possession/attributive
- Partitive: most often with a word of quantity, where something is uncountable or abstract
- Descriptive (of quality)
- After certain adjectives, nouns and verbs
- Characterising
- In front of certain postpositions

#### Dative

- Indirect object
- After certain verbs
- (Dis)advantage, e.g. he saved the state for the citizens
- Possession, with a part of sum
- Agent, e.g. verba nobis discenda sunt: we have to learn the words (lit. the words have to be learned by us)
- Separation
- Predicative, e.g. hic liber mihi auxilio est: this book is a source of help to me

#### Ablative

- After prepositions (usually motion away). cum + abl. is accompaniment.
- Agent or instrument. An agent (usually a person) takes a/ab with the ablative.
- Ablative absolute. A present participle only ends in -e in an ablative absolute.
- Time when and time within which
- Description (physical)
- Comparison
- Price
- Manner: generally with cum, but not necessarily, particularly when the Latin would become clunky

#### Locative

- A separate case for domus (home), towns, cities and small islands. It shows location without a preposition.
- It looks like the genitive in the 1st and 2nd declension singular, and the ablative in the 3rd declension singular.

## Adjectives & adverbs

> 2-1-2 and 3-3, comparatives, superlatives, adverbs

### 2-1-2 adjectives

- e.g. bonus (good), laetus (happy), stultus (foolish). They decline exactly like 1st and 2nd declension nouns.
- They come after the noun unless they define size or quality.
- pulcher (beautiful) ends in -r in the masculine nominative singular, becomes pulchra and pulchrum in the feminine and neuter, then declines as normal.
- Some, like miser (sad), keep the -er in the stem throughout: miser, misera, miserum.
- uterque also declines like pulcher, with the endings changing before the -que.

### 3-3 adjectives

- Most decline like omnis (all) and tristis (sad); some decline like ingens (huge). The stem is given by the genitive.

The main differences from 3rd declension nouns:

- The ablative singular ends in -i instead of -e
- The genitive plural is always -ium
- The neuter nom./voc./acc. singular ends in -e (for some adjectives)
- The neuter nom./voc./acc. plural ends in -ia

Also:

- The present participle (e.g. amans, loving) declines like a 3-3 adjective, but it can end in -e in the ablative singular when used in an ablative absolute.
- A mixed group, such as totus (whole) and nullus (none), decline 2-1-2 except in the genitive and dative singular, which behave like a pronoun (-ius and -i).

### Comparatives

- All comparatives decline like a 3-3 adjective, but the ablative singular still ends in -e.
- They end in -ior, added to the basic stem: laetus → laetior.

- Comparison is often expressed with quam (than), with both things compared in the same case.
- Alternatively, leave out quam and put the second noun in the ablative.
- multo (by much) stresses the difference: hic puer multo stultior est quam frater (this boy is much more stupid than his brother).
- Comparative adverbs use the neuter singular of the comparative adjective.

### Superlatives

- Superlatives decline like a 2-1-2 adjective, with no irregular endings. Added to the stem is one of:
    - -issim- (most adjectives)
    - -errim- (adjectives ending in -er)
    - -illim- (only six adjectives in -lis: facilis, difficilis, similis, dissimilis, gracilis, humilis). Other adjectives in -is take -issim-: tristis → tristissimus.

- For complimentary allusions, the superlative is used: Crassus, civis amplissimus (Crassus, a distinguished citizen).
- In favourable allusions vir is used; in unfavourable ones, homo.
- A proper noun can’t be qualified by an adjective, nor can a common noun used ‘properly’.

### Adverbs

- Add -e to the stem of a 2-1-2 adjective.
- Add -iter to the stem of a 3-3 adjective.
    - Occasionally the -i- is dropped: audax → audacter.
    - Some 3-3 adjectives in -is end in -e: facilis → facile.
- Comparative adverbs use the neuter of the comparative adjective.
- Superlative adverbs replace the adjectival ending with -e.

### Possessives

- meus (my), tuus (your), suus (his), noster (our) and vester (your) can be used as adjectives in place of the genitive of the personal pronoun.
- They agree in number and case with the thing possessed, not the possessor.
- meus, tuus and suus decline as 2-1-2 adjectives (meus, mea, meum). noster and vester also decline 2-1-2, but like pulcher (noster, nostra, nostrum), with the stems nostr- and vestr-.
- suus (an adjective) is only used to refer back to the subject. Otherwise eius (not an adjective; the genitive of is, ea, id) is used.

### Interrogatives

- The interrogative pronoun (who? what?) is identical to the relative, apart from the nominative singular (quis, quis, quid) and the neuter accusative singular (quid).
- The interrogative adjective is identical to qui, and agrees with a noun to mean ‘which [noun]?’

### Pronouns based on qui and quis

- quidam (a certain, some): declines like idem, with -m becoming -n to aid pronunciation.
- aliquis (someone, something): like quis with the ali- prefix, except the feminine nominative singular, which is aliqua.
- quisquam (anyone, anything): only ever after a negative. Declines like quis, except the neuter singular is quic- instead of quid-.
- quisque (each, every): like aliquis, but the feminine singular is quaeque.
- quisquis (whoever, whatever): both parts decline, but the first quid in the neuter singular becomes quic: quicquid.
- quicumque (whoever, whatever): declines normally like qui.

## Prepositions

> Which case each one takes

- Prepositions are followed by the accusative or the ablative, and come immediately before the noun they govern.
- Prepositions taking the accusative often suggest motion towards; those taking the ablative suggest a position of rest or motion away from.
- in, sub and super can take either case (marked ✱).
- Some prepositions can be prefixed to verbs to form compounds (egredior, ingredior, etc.).
- causa (for the sake of) is a postposition and governs the genitive.

## Verbs

> Tenses, conjugations, endings, imperatives, infinitives, participles

### Tenses, moods and voices

| Tense | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Future | I shall play |
| Future perfect | I will have played |
| Present | I play, I am playing |
| Imperfect | I was playing, I used to play, I began to play |
| Perfect | (normally) I played; (true perfect) I have played |
| Pluperfect | I had played |

- The future, future perfect, present and true perfect create a clause in primary sequence; the imperfect, perfect and pluperfect create a clause in historic sequence.
- Three moods: indicative, imperative, subjunctive.
- Two voices: active and passive. Deponent verbs are passive in form but active in meaning.

### Conjugations

- 1st: characteristic vowel -a in the indicative.
- 2nd: -e.
- 3rd: -e/-i (irregular).
- 4th: -ie.
- Mixed (3½, or 3.9 as SY calls it): follows the 4th conjugation, but has the infinitive and imperative of the 3rd, and a 3rd-conjugation 2nd person singular present passive (caperis).

### Active indicative endings

- Present, future and imperfect: present stem + characteristic vowel + ending. In the present 1st sg., -o replaces the vowel. The 3rd and 4th conjugation future takes the present stem + ending, with no characteristic vowel.
- Perfect, pluperfect and future perfect: perfect stem + ending. The perfect stem is everything up to and including the -v- (portav-). 2nd and 3rd conjugation perfect stems are often irregular and have to be learned separately.
- venio and its compounds (4th conjugation) have the perfect stem ven-, not veniv-.
- The imperfect is recognisable by the -ba-.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sg. | -o | -bo | -am | -bam | -i | -eram | -ero |
| 2nd sg. | -s | -bis | -es | -bas | -isti | -eras | -eris |
| 3rd sg. | -t | -bit | -et | -bat | -it | -erat | -erit |
| 1st pl. | -mus | -bimus | -emus | -bamus | -imus | -eramus | -erimus |
| 2nd pl. | -tis | -bitis | -etis | -batis | -istis | -eratis | -eritis |
| 3rd pl. | -nt | -bunt | -ent | -bant | -erunt | -erant | -erint |

|---|---|---|---|---|

- Perfect passive: PPP + present of sum (portatus sum).
- Pluperfect passive: PPP + imperfect of sum (portatus eram).
- Future perfect passive: PPP + future of sum (portatus ero).
- In the plural, the participle’s ending changes to fit the number (portati sumus).

### Subjunctive

- Present: present stem + subjunctive vowel + ending. The vowels are 1st -e, 2nd -ea, 3rd -a, 4th and mixed -ia.
- Active endings (the same for all active subjunctives): -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt.
- Passive endings: -r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -mini, -ntur.
- Imperfect: present infinitive + endings (portarem, portarer).
- Perfect active: perfect stem + -eri- + endings. It is identical to the future perfect indicative except in the 1st person singular (-erim, not -ero).
- Pluperfect active: perfect infinitive + endings (portavissem).
- Perfect passive: PPP + present subjunctive of sum (portatus sim).
- Pluperfect passive: PPP + imperfect subjunctive of sum (portatus essem).
- Periphrastic future: future active participle + present subjunctive of sum (portaturus sim). Very rarely seen, as the future is often represented by a present subjunctive. It can appear in indirect questions and result clauses.
- Future-in-the-past: future active participle + imperfect subjunctive of sum (portaturus essem). Used like the periphrastic future, but in historic sequence.
- In all the compound forms, the participle changes to fit the number.

- The deponent singular imperative is the same as the active infinitive of its conjugation.
- 2nd person negative: noli/nolite + infinitive.
- 1st or 3rd person negative: ne + subjunctive.
- Irregular singular imperatives: dic (dico), duc (duco), fer (fero), fac (facio). Remember: ‘dic the duc had fer on his back and that’s a fac(t)’.

- eo: i, plural ite.

Future imperative (ignore for A level), used when there is a distinct reference to the future:

| Conjugation | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | Mixed | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Singular | -ato | -eto | -ito | -ito | -ito |
| Plural | -atote | -etote | -itote | -itote | -itote |

### Infinitives

| Conjugation | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | Mixed | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active | -are | -ere | -ere | -ere | -ire |
| Passive | -ari | -eri | -i | -i | -iri |

- The 3rd and mixed conjugations have a passive infinitive in -i, not -eri.
- Perfect: perfect stem + -isse (active); PPP + esse (passive).
- Future: future active participle + esse (active); supine + iri (passive), or fore ut + subjunctive (usually in reported speech, and for verbs with no supine). fore is the abbreviated future infinitive of sum.
- In the plural, the participle’s ending changes to fit the number. The supine never changes its ending.

### Participles

- Participles are verbal adjectives describing a noun. They show when an action happens relative to the main verb.
- Present (active only): happening strictly at the same time as the main verb. Present stem + characteristic vowel + -ns (portans, stem portant-). Declines like ingens, but the ablative singular ends in -e in an ablative absolute.
    - With part of is, ea, id it suggests a more defined noun (those men fleeing).
- Perfect: happened before the main verb. It is the 4th principal part, and declines exactly like a 2-1-2 adjective. Normal verbs make a perfect passive participle; deponents make a perfect active participle.
- Future (active only): will happen after the main verb. The stem of the past participle with -ur- before regular 2-1-2 endings (portaturus).

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present | portans | – |
| Perfect | hortatus (deponent) | portatus |
| Future | portaturus | – |

- Full forms are on pp. 273–274 in the textbook.
- sum (I am), possum (I am able), eo (I go), volo (I want), nolo (I do not want), malo (I prefer), fio (I become), fero (I bring).

## Sequence & negation

> Sequence of tenses, non and ne, ‘any’ after negatives

### Sequence of tenses

- The tense of the subjunctive in a subordinate clause is set by the tense of the main verb, and by when the subjunctive action happens relative to the main verb.

| Main verb | Same time or after | Before | Future |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: present, future, future perfect, true perfect, imperative | present | perfect | -urus sim |
| Historic: imperfect, perfect, pluperfect | imperfect | pluperfect | -urus essem |

- The true perfect is like the Ancient Greek perfect: it describes a present state (I have played).

### Negation

- non negates a fact and usually goes with the indicative.
- non usually comes before the verb; otherwise it negates the following word.
- non is used with the subjunctive in negative result clauses (ut… non), cum clauses, deliberative questions, and the apodosis of closed conditionals.
- ne negates a possibility and goes with the subjunctive.
- ne replaces ut in a negative purpose clause or indirect command.
- In a fearing clause, ne is translated positively (‘that’).
- Negative conditionals use nisi in the protasis and non in the apodosis.
- Unlike Greek, a double negative expresses a positive (like English).
- Negative direct commands use noli/nolite + infinitive.
- As et non becomes neque, et ne becomes neve.

### ‘Any’ after negatives

- Post A level, but it never hurts to know.
- After a negative word, ‘any’ is quisquam (adjective ullus). After ne, ‘any’ becomes the indefinite quis (ne quis).

| Introductory negative | non | nec/neque/noli… | ne |
|---|---|---|---|
| No one | nemo | nec quisquam | ne quis |
| Nothing | nihil | nec quidquam | ne quid |
| No (adj.) | nullus | neque ullus | ne qui/quae/quod |
| Nowhere | nusquam | neque usquam | necubi |
| Never | numquam | neque umquam | ne quando |

- nescio quis (and all equivalents in the indefinite sense) do not take a subjunctive.

## Main clauses

> Questions, commands, prohibitions, exhortations, wishes

### Direct questions

- Yes/no questions add -ne to the first word of the sentence. The first word is almost always the verb, unless another word is emphasised.
- If a certain answer is expected, nonne is used for ‘yes’ and num for ‘no’.
- Questions asking for specific information use an interrogative word, usually beginning qu- (quantus, quis).

### Deliberative questions

- A present or imperfect subjunctive in place of an indicative as the main verb. The negative is non.
- num and nonne are not used. For ‘whether… or…’, use utrum… an.

### Direct commands

- 2nd person: imperative. A vocative may be present, but isn’t necessary.
- 1st or 3rd person: jussive subjunctive.

### Prohibitions

- 2nd person: imperative of nolo + infinitive.
- 1st or 3rd person: ne + subjunctive.
- You can’t join two prohibitions with et noli (it doesn’t exist). If the second is negative, use ne + subjunctive.
- For ‘neither… nor…’ use neve… neve…. If only the second phrase is negative, use neque.

### Exhortations

- A 1st or 3rd person command (‘let us do X’, ‘let them do X’): a present jussive subjunctive as the main verb.

### Wishes

- utinam (‘if only’, ‘I wish that’) + subjunctive:
    - for the future: present subjunctive
    - for the present: imperfect subjunctive
    - for the past: pluperfect subjunctive
- The negative uses ne.

## Infinitive, participle & subjunctive

> What each one does

### Uses of the infinitive

- An infinitive is technically an indeclinable neuter noun.
- Main uses:
    - governed by certain verbs (I am able to dance)
    - indirect statement, with the accusative
    - indirect command after iubeo and veto
- OCR’s names for the other uses (you could probably get away with not knowing them):
    - As subject: dolere malum est (to suffer pain is an evil). This is the neuter noun use.
    - As complement: follows a word where you’d often have an adjective as a nominative complement: audere est facere (to dare is to do). facere is a nominative complement to audere, the subject.
    - Prolative: similar to the complement, but instead of being a nominative complement, it acts like an accusative gerund. Follows words which take an infinitive in English: debeo currere (I ought to run).
    - Historic: an infinitive in place of an imperfect: clamare omnes (everyone began to shout). Uncommon in unseens, but seen in the prose set texts.
- In verse, a syncopated perfect may look like an infinitive (amaverunt → amavere), but it isn’t one.

### Uses of the participle

- As an adjective: pons cadens (the falling bridge).
- Translated as a main verb, though it is almost never the main verb: vir secutus militem interfecit (lit. the man, having followed the soldier, killed [him]; better: the man followed and killed the soldier).
- Ablative absolute: a noun or pronoun and a participle, both in the ablative. Translate flexibly: when, since, because, after, despite, although.
    - sum has no present participle, so it is left out: Caesare duce (with Caesar as the leader).
    - It can only be used if it can be removed without affecting the rest of the sentence (hence absolute). If it refers to something in the main clause, it can’t be an ablative absolute.

### Subjunctive as a main verb

- Potential: a possibility rather than a fact, often translated ‘may’ or ‘might’.
- Wish for the future (optative; not to be confused with the Greek mood). Negative ne.
- Jussive (see direct commands).
- Deliberative question. Negative non.

### Subjunctive in subordinate clauses

- Purpose clauses, after ut or qui.
- Indirect commands, after verbs such as impero, with ut.
- Fearing clauses, after a verb of fearing (timeo, vereor, metuo) or the nouns metus and periculum (with est or erat: there is a fear that… there is a danger that…), with ne.
    - Fears for the present or future take a present or imperfect subjunctive (by sequence); fears for the past take a perfect or pluperfect (by sequence).
    - The negative is ne… non.
- Purpose clauses and indirect commands only take the present or imperfect subjunctive, by sequence.
- Result clauses: a signpost word (tam, adeo) + ut. Tense by sense:
    - present: a recurrent outcome, or something still true
    - imperfect: a generally recurrent outcome in the past
    - perfect: a single outcome in the past
    - The negative is ut… non, not ne.
    - se refers to the subject of the result clause, not the subject of the main verb.
    - After a comparative, quam ut gives a hypothetical result: X is too Y to do Z.
- Generic relative: qui, quae, quod + subjunctive, describing the type of person likely to do something.
- Indirect questions: an interrogative word, or num (whether…?) for yes/no questions.
    - ‘whether… or…’ takes utrum… an….
    - nescio an and haud scio an make a modest assertion (I rather think…).
    - Extended sequence of tenses applies: the tense of the original question rules the tense of the subjunctive.
    - They can be introduced by words that don’t obviously ask a question (‘understanding what…’).
    - Interrogative words are the same as in direct questions, apart from cur (becomes quare) and quomodo (becomes quem ad modum).
    - ‘any’ in a question (anyone, anything) is num quis, num quid, or the equivalent.
- Closed conditionals, after si. The apodosis is always translated with ‘would’.
- Subordinate clauses in extended indirect speech (conditionals, relative clauses, temporal clauses). Generally, any indicative in a subordinate clause becomes subjunctive in extended indirect speech.
- Some temporal clauses, mainly after cum (when, since, although) or dum (until, when there is a sense of purpose; so long as).

## Gerunds & gerundives

> Formation, uses, gerundival attraction

### Gerund

- A noun made from a verb: ‘the act of doing X’.
- Present stem + characteristic vowel + -ndum. Like infinitives, gerunds are neuter nouns, declining like bellum. There is no plural.

| Case | Form |
|---|---|
| Nom. | amare |
| Acc. | amare/amandum |
| Gen. | amandi |
| Dat. | amando |
| Abl. | amando |

- In the nominative, the gerund is just the infinitive.
- In the accusative, the infinitive is used as a direct object; otherwise it’s the -ndum form, such as after prepositions.
- ad + accusative gerund often shows purpose. ad + gerund can’t take an object.
- The genitive is often used with the postposition causa to express purpose. It also follows various nouns and adjectives: spes discendi (the hope of learning).
- Dative, with a ‘causal’ meaning: utilis navigando (helpful for sailing).
- Ablative, almost instrumental: effugi currendo (I escaped by running).
- Gerunds can be qualified by adverbs, since they are still technically part of a verb.

### Gerundive

- A verbal adjective, like a participle. Always passive, even from a deponent, and carries the idea of obligation: ‘needing to be X-ed’.
- Formed like the gerund, with 2-1-2 endings.

| Case | m | f | n |
|---|---|---|---|
| Singular | | | |
| Nom. | amandus | amanda | amandum |
| Voc. | amandus | amanda | amandum |
| Acc. | amandum | amandam | amandum |
| Gen. | amandi | amandae | amandi |
| Dat. | amando | amandae | amando |
| Abl. | amando | amanda | amando |
| Plural | | | |
| Nom. | amandi | amandae | amanda |
| Voc. | amandi | amandae | amanda |
| Acc. | amandos | amandas | amanda |
| Gen. | amandorum | amandarum | amandorum |
| Dat. | amandis | amandis | amandis |
| Abl. | amandis | amandis | amandis |

- With a part of sum, it expresses obligation: liber legendus est (the book must be read).
- A gerundive with the perfect subjunctive of esse is used as the future perfect passive subjunctive.
- The agent goes in the dative: liber mihi legendus est (the book must be read by me; I must read the book).
- If a second dative would cause ambiguity, use a/ab + ablative for the agent: pecunia a me tibi reddenda est (I need to return the money to you).
- With curo (I get something done) or suscipio (I undertake), the gerundive agrees with the object and is translated as a gerund: imperator moenia aedificanda curavit (the general looked after the building of the walls).
- In the genitive, it follows words like ars (skill), potestas (ability), occasio (opportunity), spes (hope), cupidus (desirous/greedy).
- Purpose: ad + gerundive agreeing with the accusative object: ad agricolas adiuvandos (to help the farmers). causa + genitive also works.
- Intransitive verbs, or verbs taking another case, use the gerundive impersonally: parendum est legibus (the laws must be obeyed).

### Gerundival attraction

- A Latin gerund doesn’t take a direct object (unlike English). Instead, a gerundive agrees with the noun or pronoun that would have been the object: urbis videndae causa profecti sumus (we set out for the sake of seeing the city).
- Exceptions:
    - If the gerundival phrase would be genitive plural, a gerund may govern a direct object: amicos videndi causa pervenimus rather than amicorum videndorum causa pervenimus.
    - A gerund may govern a neuter pronoun object: senatoribus haec dicendo persuasit rather than senatoribus his dicendis persuasit.
- ad + gerundive is gerundival attraction with no case change: pugnavimus ad hostes vincendos.

## Subordinate clauses

> Indirect statement, relative, conditional, temporal, causal, concessive, purpose, result

### Indirect statement

- Accusative + infinitive, introduced by a verb of speaking, thinking or perception. The subject is accusative.
- If the subject is the same as that of the introducing verb, se is used.
- The negative is nego rather than dico… non.
- If the introducing verb is passive, the subject is nominative.
- Even if the introducing verb doesn’t normally take an accusative, the subject of the indirect statement is still accusative.
- (Post A level) If a reported clause has a subordinate clause depending on it, that verb goes into the subjunctive: quod vera dixissem, negavit se me puniturum esse (because I had told the truth, he said that he would not punish me).

### Relative clauses

- Introduced by part of qui, quae, quod, with an indicative verb.
- The relative agrees with its antecedent in number and gender; its case comes from its job in the relative clause.
- Complex structures:
    - A superlative that would describe the antecedent is drawn into the relative clause and describes the relative pronoun instead.
    - If the antecedent is a pronoun in the same case, it is sometimes left out.
    - The antecedent can be absorbed into the relative clause and take the case of the relative pronoun.
    - The relative clause can come before the antecedent.
- A relative pronoun after a full stop or semicolon is a connecting relative: translate it as part of is, ea, id or hic, haec, hoc.

### quin

- If the main clause and the relative clause are both negative, qui… non becomes quin. This only applies in the masculine nominative singular; all other rules stay the same.

### Conditional clauses

- Introduced by si, or nisi in the negative.
- Open conditionals take the indicative: future for a future condition, present for present, any past tense for past. The tenses can be mixed, so translate accordingly.
    - The apodosis may be an imperative or jussive subjunctive.
    - Beware the hidden future: English may use a present for an immediate future event, but Latin strictly uses the future.
- Closed conditionals take the subjunctive, moved one tense on: present subjunctive for a future condition, imperfect for present, pluperfect for past.
- Double conditionals use seu… seu… (whether… or…) before a consonant, otherwise sive… sive…. sive minus may be used for ‘if not’.
- For ‘any’, quis and related words are used.

### Temporal clauses

- Generally a temporal word (such as ubi) + indicative, in the same tense as English.
- When ubi, postquam or simulac refer to the past and introduce an earlier action, a perfect is used in place of a pluperfect.
- Time clauses referring to the future often have a hidden future in English.
- A subjunctive implies another idea, such as purpose, as well as time.
- dum:
    - ‘while’: present indicative when an ongoing action is interrupted; imperfect indicative when there are two ongoing actions.
    - ‘until’: the normal rules of temporal clauses. With a sense of purpose, a subjunctive by sequence.
    - ‘so long as’ or ‘provided that’: dummodo + subjunctive by sequence.
- cum (when, since, because) + imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive. An inverted cum clause works the same way, but the cum clause comes after the main clause.

### Causal clauses

- Generally quod, but also quia or quoniam, + indicative. If the reason is only alleged, subjunctive.
- With both a rejected (alleged) reason and the real one: non quod… sed quia….
- A relative clause can be causal (‘since’); the verb is then subjunctive. quippe is occasionally placed before the relative pronoun.
- To emphasise the reason rather than the main verb: ideo… quod (it is because… that).

### Concessive clauses

- quamquam or etsi + indicative: a concession of fact.
- cum + subjunctive can also be used.
- A clause referring to possibility takes the subjunctive: quamvis with an adjective or adverb, otherwise etsi/etiam si.
- licet can replace quamvis as a conjunction with the subjunctive (‘granted that…’).
- Concessive relative clauses take the subjunctive, like causal ones.

### Purpose clauses

- ut (ne in the negative) + subjunctive by sequence of tenses.
- With iubeo, the negative becomes veto.
- There are many other ways to convey purpose or obligation (see the gerund and gerundive sections).

### Result clauses

- A signpost word, then ut + subjunctive. The tense is set by sense, not by sequence:
    - present: a recurrent action, or a result that is still true
    - imperfect: the result of an action in the past
    - perfect: a single outcome that was achieved
- The negative is ut… non, not ne.

| Signpost | How it works |
|---|---|
| tam (so) | qualifies an adjective or adverb |
| talis, -e (of such a kind) | adjective |
| totiens (so often) | adverb |
| tantus, -a, -um (so great) | adjective, essentially ‘tam magnus’ |
| tot (so many) | adjective, indeclinable |
| ita (in this way) | adverb |
| sic (thus) | adverb |
| adeo (to such an extent) | adverb |

### Comparison idioms

- Sooner than expected: spe (+ gen.) celerius. Larger than was thought: opinione (+ gen.) maior.
- With numbers, plus, amplius and minus don’t need a word for ‘than’.
- When two adjectives are compared (more lucky than careful), both are comparative in Latin.
- Ratio sentences (the more… the more…): quo + comparative… eo + comparative.
- ‘much x-er’: multo + comparative. ‘by far the x-est’: longe + superlative.
- In negative sentences, ‘very’ is translated by a comparative rather than a superlative.

### Types of clause

- Clauses can be noun, adverb or adjective clauses, depending on how they act. A relative clause is an adjective clause (it describes a noun), but a relative purpose clause is a noun clause.

## Metre

> Hexameter, elegiac couplets, long and short syllables

- Ovid (the verse author for 2023) wrote the Metamorphoses in hexameter; as far as I know, all his other works are in elegiac couplets.
- Check scansion with Pedecerto.

### Hexameter

- The metre of epic. Each line has six feet, each a dactyl (– ⏑ ⏑) or a spondee (– –).

To scan a line:

1. Check for elisions. If a word ends in a vowel or -m and the next word begins with a vowel or h, the final syllable of the first word is elided (put it in brackets).
2. Mark in the final two feet. The fifth foot is almost always a dactyl; the sixth is a long syllable followed by an anceps (x, either long or short). Think ‘strawberry jam jar’.
3. Count the remaining syllables. This tells you how many feet are dactyls and how many are spondees.
4. Identify the obviously long and short syllables.

### Long and short syllables

- Long:
    - the first syllable of the line
    - diphthongs
    - a vowel followed by two consonants
- Notes on consonants:
    - u is ignored after q, c or g.
    - i is a consonant at the start of a word, and when followed by a vowel (Iulius).
    - h doesn’t count as a consonant.
    - Muta cum liquida: l and r are not always counted as consonants when they follow another consonant (ll and rr always count).
- Short: the first of two consecutive, separately sounded vowels in a word.
- Some vowels are short or long by nature (not essential to know):

| Ending | Usually | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| -a | short (1st decl. nom. sg., neuter nom./acc. pl.) | long in 1st decl. abl. sg. |
| -e | short | long in 5th decl. abl. sg. |
| -i | long | either in mihi, tibi, sibi, ubi, quasi, nisi |
| -o | long | short in ego, modo, duo, puto, scio, nescio |
| -u | always long | |
| -am, -em, -um | always short | |
| -is | short | long in 1st and 2nd decl. dat./abl. pl. |
| -us | short | long in 4th decl. gen. sg. and nom./acc. pl. |
| -as, -es, -os | long | -es is short in some 3rd decl. nom. sg. (miles) |

### Elegiac couplets

- A line of hexameter followed by a line of pentameter.
- Rather than five feet, think of the pentameter as two lots of two and a half feet.
- The final eight syllables of a pentameter always scan the same way: – | – ⏑ ⏑ | – ⏑ ⏑ | x. Think ‘hot dog, tator tot, casserole’.
- The first two feet scan as normal.

## Unseen & prose tips

> Personal tips for translation and composition

### Unseen translation

- Use the English! There are always a few lines of English for context: use them for vocab and for a general idea of what’s going on. In verse, there are generally around three major points.
- If word order is throwing you off, go very literally. Translating each word by itself can give you a general idea of what’s going on.
- If in doubt about how to translate something, stay literal.
- Sometimes reading ahead to the next sentence gives enough context to fill in the blanks.
- For words you don’t know, try to derive a meaning from a cognate or from context.
- Don’t jump ahead in guessing what’s going on; it can lead you down a slippery slope and waste time.
- Imperatives are much more common in verse, so be careful not to mix them up with nouns.
- Split sentences (or lines) at hard punctuation, then separate them into clauses where possible.
- Marking in agreements beforehand never hurts.
- Ovid likes a trio of noun + adjective + another noun in the ablative, which should be translated in that order (I see the gate posts marked out by gleaming arms).
- In verse, scan the lines to tell apart cases that look ambiguous.
- Livy likes long periods (long sentences with many subordinate clauses, where the whole meaning only arrives at the end). Feel free to split them into shorter sentences.
- Livy may also omit parts of sum, so be careful to find the main verb.

### Prose composition

- Use any context where possible.
- If you’re unsure how to translate a phrase or word, find a synonymous phrase or word to replace it.
- Watch the agreements carefully.
- Work out exactly which constructions you need, and form each word carefully.
- Annotate!
- Don’t rush into chasing style marks; it’s better to be literally correct than to make a mistake going for an extra mark.
- Style marks can come from (not exhaustive):
    - putting the nominative at the start, before subordinate clauses
    - correct word order (especially connectives that come second, e.g. tamen, enim, igitur)
    - subordination
    - connecting relatives
