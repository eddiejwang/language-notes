## The article

> The definite article, translating ‘a’, the article as a pronoun

### Definite article

- Like English, Greek uses the definite article (the). A noun without one is translated ‘a …’: ὁ δουλος (the slave), δουλος (a slave).
- Proper nouns usually take the article too (ὁ Σωκρατης).
- There is no indefinite article. τις (a certain, some) can do the job, coming after its noun: ἀνηρ τις (a certain man).

### Using the article

- With an adjective, adverb or participle, the article makes a noun: οἱ ἐλευθεροι (the free [men]), οἱ νυν (the men of today), οἱ φευγοντες (the fleeing [men]).
- With an infinitive it makes a verbal noun: το φιλοσοφειν (philosophising).
- With a genitive, the noun is often left out: οἱ του Κυρου (Cyrus’ men), τα της πολεως (the city’s affairs).
- ὁ μεν… ὁ δε… means ‘the one… the other…’; οἱ μεν… οἱ δε… means ‘some… others…’.
- ὁ δε (or ἡ δε) at the start of a clause means ‘and he’ (or ‘and she’), and usually marks a change of subject.
- Beyond A Level, certain phrases don’t use an article (e.g. in the agora).

### 1st declension

- Feminine nouns in -α keep the α all through the singular if the stem ends in ε, ι or ρ (χωρα). Otherwise the genitive and dative singular switch to η (θαλασσα, θαλασσης).
- A few 1st declension nouns are masculine (like Latin, often jobs). They end in -ης or -ας, have a genitive singular in -ου and a vocative in -α, and take the masculine article:
    - κριτης, κριτου (judge)
    - ναυτης, ναυτου (sailor)
    - νεανιας, νεανιου (young man)
- θαλασσα and θαλαττα are the same word; -σσ- or -ττ- is a dialect choice (Attic prose uses -ττ-).

### 2nd declension

- The masculine vocative singular ends in -ε.
- Feminine nouns such as νησος (island) and ὁδος (road) decline the same way, but take the feminine article.
- νους (mind) and πλους (voyage) are contracted: νους, νουν, νου, νῳ (from νοος etc.).

### 3rd declension

- The nominative varies a lot, but from the accusative singular onwards the endings are regular, added to a stem that is often different from the nominative.
- The genitive singular gives you the stem: φυλαξ, φυλακος → φυλακ-.
- There is a movable nu (ν) on the dative plural (and on some verb endings) before a vowel or at the end of a sentence.

### Uses of the cases

#### Nominative

- Subject of the verb
- In apposition to the subject
- Complement, often after a part of εἰμι

#### Vocative

- Addressing someone, usually after ὦ: ὦ ἀνδρες Ἀθηναιοι (men of Athens)

#### Accusative

- Direct object of the verb
- After certain prepositions (usually motion towards)
- Subject of the infinitive in indirect statement
- Accusative of respect (usually body parts): ἀλγει την κεφαλην (he has a pain in his head)
- Time how long (THLACC): τρεις ἡμερας (for three days)
- Extent of space: σταθμους πεντε (five days’ march)
- Double accusative with verbs of asking, teaching and depriving: διδασκω τους παιδας την μουσικην (I teach the boys music)
- Adverbial: τι; (why?), οὐδεν (not at all), το λοιπον (in future)
- Accusative absolute (with impersonal verbs; see Infinitive & participle)

#### Genitive

- Possession/attributive
- Partitive, most often with a word of quantity: πολλοι των στρατιωτων (many of the soldiers)
- With prepositions (usually motion away)
- Genitive absolute
- Comparison: μειζων του πατρος (bigger than his father)
- Time within which (TWIGEN): νυκτος (during the night), δεκα ἡμερων (within ten days)
- Agent with ὑπο
- Patronymic: Θουκυδιδης ὁ Ὀλορου (Thucydides, son of Olorus)
- Price or value: πεντε δραχμων (for five drachmas)
- Separation: ἀπεχει της πολεως (he is away from the city)
- After verbs of hearing (a person), remembering, forgetting, ruling, aiming at, sharing and touching: ἀκουω του Σωκρατους (I hear Socrates), ἀρχει της πολεως (he rules the city)

#### Dative

- Indirect object
- Instrument: λιθοις βαλλειν (to pelt with stones)
- With verbs of trusting, obeying, helping, following, using and fighting: πιστευω, πειθομαι, βοηθεω, ἑπομαι, χραομαι, μαχομαι
- Sometimes the passive agent (instead of ὑπο + gen.), especially with the perfect passive
- Possession, with εἰμι: ἐστι μοι βιβλος (I have a book)
- Place, mostly with place names: Μαραθωνι (at Marathon)
- Time when (TWIT): τῃ ὑστεραιᾳ (on the next day)
- Agent with the verbal adjective in -τεος
- Manner: σιγῃ (in silence), σπουδῃ (hastily)
- Cause: φοβῳ (through fear)
- Measure of difference, with comparatives: πολλῳ μειζων (much bigger)
- Respect: ἀσθενης τῳ σωματι (weak in body)
- Accompaniment, often with αὐτος: αὐτοις τοις ἱπποις (horses and all)

## Adjectives & adverbs

> 2-1-2, 3-1-3 and 3-3 adjectives, word order, comparison, adverbs

### 2-1-2 adjectives

- e.g. σοφος (wise), καλος (beautiful), κακος (bad). They decline like λογος, τιμη and δωρον.
- If the stem ends in ε, ι or ρ, the feminine singular declines like χωρα instead of τιμη: δικαιος, δικαια, δικαιον (just).
- Some compound adjectives (with a prefix, or more than one element), such as ἀδικος (unjust) and βαρβαρος (barbarian), have no separate feminine. These are 2-2 adjectives.

### Word order: attributive and predicative

- Attributive (describing the noun): between the article and the noun, or after the noun with the article repeated: ὁ σοφος ἀνηρ or ὁ ἀνηρ ὁ σοφος (the wise man).
- Predicative (making a statement): outside the article–noun group: ὁ ἀνηρ σοφος or σοφος ὁ ἀνηρ (the man is wise).
- This changes the meaning of some words:
    - ἡ μεση πολις (the middle city), but μεση ἡ πολις (the middle of the city); ἀκρος works the same way (the top of)
    - πασα ἡ πολις (the whole city), ἡ πασα πολις (the city as a whole), πασα πολις (every city)
    - αὐτος and the demonstratives (see Pronouns)

### Comparatives

- Most comparatives decline like a 2-1-2 adjective. Add -τερος to the masculine stem.
- The ο before -τερος becomes ω if the syllable before it is short: σοφος → σοφωτερος, but δεινος → δεινοτερος.
- Many irregular comparatives end in -ων and decline like μειζων.
- Comparison is often expressed by ἠ (than), with both things compared in the same case.
- Alternatively, leave out ἠ and put the second noun in the genitive.
- On its own, a comparative can mean ‘rather’ or ‘too’: ἀνδρειοτερος (rather brave).

### Superlatives

- All superlatives decline like a 2-1-2 adjective. They are formed like the comparative, but with -τατος instead of -τερος: σοφωτατος.
- Irregular superlatives often end in -ιστος.
- ὡς or ὁτι + superlative means ‘as … as possible’: ὡς ταχιστα (as quickly as possible).

### Adverbs

- Most adverbs are formed by changing the -ων of the genitive plural to -ως: σοφων → σοφως (wisely), ἀληθων → ἀληθως (truly), ταχεων → ταχεως (quickly).
- Irregular: εὖ (well), from ἀγαθος; μαλα (very), μαλλον (more, rather), μαλιστα (most, especially).
- Comparative adverbs use the neuter singular of the comparative adjective (σοφωτερον).
- Superlative adverbs use the neuter plural of the superlative adjective (σοφωτατα).

### αὐτος

αὐτος has three meanings depending on word order:

- Self: in the nominative on its own, or in predicative position: αὐτος ὁ βασιλευς (the king himself).
- The same: in attributive position, between the article and the noun: ὁ αὐτος βασιλευς (the same king). With a dative, ‘the same as’: την αὐτην γνωμην ἐχω σοι (I have the same opinion as you).
- Him, her, it, them: on its own, not in the nominative.

### Possessives

- ἐμος (my), σος (your), ἡμετερος (our), ὑμετερος (your, pl.) decline 2-1-2 and go in attributive position: ὁ ἐμος πατηρ (my father).
- For ‘his, her, their’ use the genitive: αὐτου if it isn’t the subject’s (ὁ πατηρ αὐτου, his father), ἑαυτου if it is (τον ἑαυτου πατερα, his own father).

### Demonstratives

- οὑτος means ‘this’, often referring back to something already mentioned.
- ὁδε means ‘this here’, or ‘the following’, often pointing forward.
- ἐκεινος means ‘that’.
- With a noun, they go in predicative position (outside the article–noun group), either before or after: οὑτος ὁ ἀνηρ or ὁ ἀνηρ οὑτος (this man).

## Prepositions

> Which case each one takes, set phrases, elision

- Prepositions take the accusative, genitive or dative, and come immediately before the noun they govern.
- Prepositions taking the accusative often suggest motion towards, those taking the genitive motion away from, and those taking the dative a position at rest.
- Several take more than one case, and the meaning changes with the case (marked ✱).
- Many can be prefixed to verbs to form compounds (καθιστημι, παρεχω; see Impersonal & compound verbs).
- Before a vowel, a final vowel is usually elided: δι’, ἀπ’, ἐπ’, κατ’, μετ’, παρ’, ὑπ’. Before a rough breathing, the consonant aspirates: ἀφ’, ἐφ’, καθ’, μεθ’, ὑφ’.

## Verbs

> Tenses, moods, voices, endings, augment, reduplication

### Tenses

| Tense | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Present | I stop, I am stopping |
| Future | I shall stop |
| Imperfect | I was stopping, I used to stop |
| Aorist | I stopped |
| Perfect | I have stopped |
| Pluperfect | I had stopped |

- The future perfect exists but isn’t needed for A Level.
- The aorist is either weak (1st) or strong (2nd); every verb has one or the other.
    - Weak aorist: the present stem + σ, with endings in -α (ἐπαυσα).
    - Strong aorist: a different stem, with the imperfect’s endings in the indicative (λαμβανω → ἐλαβον).
- The future, present and perfect create a clause in primary sequence; the imperfect, aorist and pluperfect create historic sequence.
- Outside the indicative, the difference between present and aorist is aspect, not time: the present is ongoing or repeated, the aorist a single act.

### Moods and voices

- Four moods: indicative, imperative, subjunctive (recognised by a long ω or η), optative (recognised by οι, αι or ει before the ending).
- Three voices: active, middle and passive. The middle is a reflexive suggestion: active παυω (I stop someone), middle παυομαι (I stop myself, I cease), passive παυομαι (I am stopped).
- The present, imperfect, perfect and pluperfect have the same forms for middle and passive; only context tells you which. The future and aorist have separate passive forms.
- Deponent verbs are middle in form but active in meaning (βουλομαι, γιγνομαι, ἐρχομαι). Some have a passive-looking aorist (βουλομαι → ἐβουληθην, δυναμαι → ἐδυνηθην).

### Augment and reduplication

- All tenses that create historic sequence begin with an augment, but only in the indicative.
    - Before a consonant, add ἐ-: παυω → ἐπαυον.
    - An initial vowel lengthens: α and ε → η, ο → ω, αι → ῃ, οι → ῳ, αυ → ηυ: ἀκουω → ἠκουον, ὁραω → ἑωρων (irregular).
    - In compound verbs the augment goes after the prefix: ἀποβαλλω → ἀπεβαλον.
- The perfect stem doubles the first consonant with ε (reduplication) and adds κ before the active endings: πε-παυ-κα.
    - An aspirated consonant reduplicates without the aspiration: θυω → τεθυκα.
    - Verbs beginning with a vowel, two consonants, or ζ, ξ, ψ lengthen or add ἐ- instead: ἀγγελλω → ἠγγελκα, στρατευω → ἐστρατευκα.
    - The middle/passive perfect has no κ: πεπαυμαι.

### Weak aorist and future stems

The σ of the future and weak aorist combines with a consonant at the end of the stem:

- π, β, φ (and πτ) + σ → ψ: πεμπω → πεμψω, ἐπεμψα
- κ, γ, χ (and ττ) + σ → ξ: διωκω → διωξω, ἐδιωξα; πραττω → πραξω
- τ, δ, θ (and ζ) + σ → σ: πειθω → πεισω, ἐπεισα
- Verbs in λ, μ, ν, ρ have no σ; the future contracts like ποιεω and the aorist lengthens the stem vowel: μενω → μενω (fut., accented μενῶ), ἐμεινα; ἀγγελλω → ἀγγελω, ἠγγειλα.

### Subjunctive

- Recognised by a long vowel (ω or η) where the indicative has a short one. If the vowel is already long, there is no change.
- Only the present and aorist are common; there is no augment.
- Active endings: -ω, -ῃς, -ῃ, -ωμεν, -ητε, -ωσι(ν).
- Middle/passive endings: -ωμαι, -ῃ, -ηται, -ωμεθα, -ησθε, -ωνται.
- The aorist passive subjunctive puts the active endings on the passive stem: παυθω, παυθῃς.
- Main uses: deliberative questions, exhortations, prohibitions (aorist), purpose and fearing clauses in primary sequence, and indefinite clauses with ἀν.

### Optative

- Recognised by οι, αι (weak aorist) or ει (aorist passive) before the ending. Present, future and aorist; no augment.
- Active: -οιμι, -οις, -οι, -οιμεν, -οιτε, -οιεν. Weak aorist: -σαιμι, -σαις, -σαι, -σαιμεν, -σαιτε, -σαιεν (with common alternatives -σειας, -σειε(ν), -σειαν).
- Middle/passive: -οιμην, -οιο, -οιτο, -οιμεθα, -οισθε, -οιντο. Weak aorist middle: -σαιμην, -σαιο, -σαιτο etc.
- Aorist passive: -θειην, -θειης, -θειη, -θειμεν, -θειτε, -θειεν.
- Main uses: wishes, the potential ‘would’ (with ἀν), future less vivid conditions, and replacing the indicative or subjunctive in historic sequence.
- The future optative only appears in indirect speech.

### Imperative

- 2nd and 3rd persons only (the 3rd person means ‘let him…’, ‘let them…’).
- Present for an ongoing or general command, aorist for a single act: λεγε (keep talking), εἰπε (say it).
- Full forms are in the Verb tables.

### Infinitives and participles

- Infinitives: present -ειν / -εσθαι; future -σειν / -σεσθαι / -θησεσθαι; aorist -σαι / -σασθαι / -θηναι; perfect -κεναι / -σθαι.
- Participles: present -ων, -ουσα, -ον; aorist -σας, -σασα, -σαν; aorist passive -θεις, -θεισα, -θεν; middle and passive -μενος, -η, -ον.
- Aorist participles generally describe an action before the main verb, present participles one at the same time, future participles one after (often purpose).

## Contract verbs

> -αω, -εω and -οω verbs

- Verbs whose stem ends in α, ε or ο contract that vowel with the ending in the present and imperfect. Every other tense is regular, with the vowel lengthened before the σ, κ or θ: τιμαω → τιμησω, ἐτιμησα; ποιεω → ποιησω; δηλοω → δηλωσω.
- The dictionary form is uncontracted (τιμαω), but you’ll only see τιμω.

### Contraction rules

- -αω: α + an e-sound (ε, η) → α; α + an o-sound (ο, ω, ου) → ω. An iota stays as a subscript (ᾳ).
- -εω: ε + ε → ει; ε + ο → ου; ε disappears before a long vowel or diphthong.
- -οω: ο + ε, ο or ου → ου; ο + η or ω → ω; ο + anything with ι → οι.
- ζαω (live) and χραομαι (use) contract to η instead of α: ζῃς, χρηται.

## Impersonal & compound verbs

> δει, χρη, ἐξεστι, accusative absolute, prefixes

### Impersonal verbs

- δει (it is necessary): accusative + infinitive: δει σε ἀπιεναι (you must leave). δει + genitive means ‘there is need of’: δει χρηματων (money is needed).
- χρη (one ought): accusative + infinitive. Imperfect χρην or ἐχρην.
- ἐξεστι (it is possible, it is allowed): dative + infinitive: ἐξεστι σοι ἀπιεναι (you may leave).
- δοκει (it seems, it seems good): dative + infinitive: ἐδοξε τοις Ἀθηναιοις πεμπειν ναυς (the Athenians decided to send ships).
- μελει (it is a concern): dative of the person + genitive of the thing: μελει μοι τουτου (I care about this).
- πρεπει and προσηκει (it is fitting): dative + infinitive.
- Accusative absolute: participles of impersonal verbs go in the neuter accusative instead of the genitive absolute: ἐξον (it being possible), δεον (it being necessary), δοξαν (it having been decided), προσηκον (it being fitting). ἐξον ἀπιεναι, ἐμεινε (although he could have left, he stayed).

### Defective verbs

- Some verbs only exist in certain tenses: οἶδα (perfect only, with present meaning), φημι (mostly present and imperfect), χρη, δει.
- Others fill gaps with other roots (ἐρχομαι, εἰμι, ἠλθον; ὁραω, ὀψομαι, εἰδον). See Principal parts.

### Compound verbs

- A prefix + a verb. The augment goes between them: ἀποβαλλω → ἀπεβαλον; προσβαλλω → προσεβαλον.
- A prefix ending in a vowel drops it before another vowel, including the augment: ἀπ-, κατ-, μετ-, παρ-, ἐπ-, ὑπ-, ἀν-, δι-. περι and προ don’t (περιεβαλον; προ often merges with the augment: προυβαλον).
- Before a rough breathing the consonant aspirates: ἀφ-, ἐφ-, καθ-, μεθ-, ὑφ- (ἀφικνεομαι, καθισταμαι).
- ἐκ becomes ἐξ before a vowel (ἐξεβαλον).
- ἐν and συν change to match the next consonant: ἐμ- and συμ- before π, β, φ, ψ, μ (ἐμπιπτω, συμβαινω); ἐγ- and συγ- before κ, γ, χ, ξ (συγγιγνωσκω); συλ- before λ; συρ- before ρ. The original form comes back before the augment: συμβαινει → συνεβη, ἐμβαλλω → ἐνεβαλον.
- Common prefixes: ἀνα (up, back), ἀπο (away, back), δια (through, apart), εἰς (into), ἐκ (out), ἐν (in), ἐπι (on, against), κατα (down, completely), μετα (change, after), παρα (beside, past), περι (around), προ (before, forward), προς (towards, in addition), συν (with, together), ὑπερ (over), ὑπο (under).

## Negation

> οὐ and μη, compound negatives

- The syntax sections follow the order of OCR’s specification. Case usage is in Nouns, comparison of adjectives in Adjectives & adverbs.
- οὐ (οὐκ before a smooth breathing, οὐχ before a rough one) negates facts and statements: the indicative, the potential optative, and the infinitive and participle in indirect statement.
- μη negates everything else: commands, prohibitions, wishes, purpose clauses, the protasis of conditionals, indefinite clauses, infinitives outside indirect statement, and participles with a conditional or general sense (οἱ μη πιστευοντες, anyone who doesn’t believe).
- Each has a family of compounds:

| οὐ form | μη form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| οὐδεις | μηδεις | no one, no |
| οὐδεν | μηδεν | nothing |
| οὐδε | μηδε | and not, not even |
| οὐτε… οὐτε | μητε… μητε | neither… nor |
| οὐκετι | μηκετι | no longer |
| οὐποτε | μηποτε | never |
| οὐδαμου | μηδαμου | nowhere |

- A compound negative after another negative strengthens it: οὐκ ἐλεγεν οὐδεις οὐδεν (nobody said anything).
- A simple negative after a compound one cancels it: οὐδεις οὐκ ἐπαθε (everyone suffered; lit. nobody did not suffer).
- μη οὐ appears after negated verbs of hindering, denying and doubting, and in fears that something won’t happen (see Subordinate clauses).
- οὐ φημι means ‘I deny’ (like Latin nego).

## Main clauses

> Statements, questions, commands, prohibitions, exhortations, wishes

### Direct questions

- Open yes/no questions: ἆρα, or just a question mark (;).
- Expecting ‘yes’: ἆρ’ οὐ or οὐ: ἆρ’ οὐ καλη ἡ πολις; (isn’t the city beautiful?)
- Expecting ‘no’: ἆρα μη, μη or μων: μη ἀπιμεν; (we aren’t leaving, are we?)
- Questions for information use a question word (τίς, ποῦ, πῶς etc.; see Correlatives in Pronouns).
- ‘Whether… or…’: ποτερον… ἠ…: ποτερον μενεις ἠ ἀπει; (will you stay or go?)

### Deliberative questions

- Subjunctive, usually 1st person: τί ποιωμεν; (what are we to do?). The negative is μη.

### Commands

- 2nd person: imperative. 3rd person: 3rd person imperative (‘let him…’): ἰτω (let him go).
- Present imperative for an ongoing or general command, aorist for a single act: λεγε (keep talking), εἰπε (say it).

### Prohibitions

- μη + present imperative: stop doing, or don’t keep doing: μη θορυβειτε (stop shouting).
- μη + aorist subjunctive: don’t (start to) do: μη θορυβησητε (don’t shout).
- Never μη + aorist imperative in the 2nd person.

### Exhortations

- 1st person (plural) subjunctive: ἰωμεν (let us go). The negative is μη: μη μενωμεν (let’s not stay).

### Wishes

- For the future: optative, often with εἰθε or εἰ γαρ: εἰθε νικησειαν (may they win!). The negative is μη.
- Unfulfilled, about the present: εἰθε or εἰ γαρ + imperfect indicative: εἰθε παρησθα (if only you were here).
- Unfulfilled, about the past: εἰθε or εἰ γαρ + aorist indicative: εἰ γαρ μη ἐνικησαν (if only they hadn’t won).
- Also ὠφελον (I ought) + infinitive for an unfulfilled wish: ὠφελε μη ἐλθειν (if only he hadn’t come).

## Infinitive & participle

> Uses of the infinitive, uses of the participle, genitive absolute

### Uses of the infinitive

- Like Latin, the infinitive is a verbal noun, but Greek can decline it using the article.
- As subject, often with το: το φιλοσοφειν καλον ἐστιν (philosophising is a fine thing). Also after impersonal verbs: δει ἀπιεναι.
- As complement, after adjectives and nouns: δεινος λεγειν (clever at speaking), ὡρα ἀπιεναι (it’s time to go).
- Prolative, completing verbs like βουλομαι, ἐθελω, δυναμαι, ἐπιχειρεω: βουλομαι μανθανειν (I want to learn). μελλω + future infinitive means ‘I am about to’.
- With the article (the articular infinitive), declining through the article: το (nom./acc.), του (gen.), τῳ (dat.):
    - δια το + infinitive: because of: δια το φοβεισθαι (because of being afraid)
    - προ του + infinitive: before
    - ἐν τῳ + infinitive: while, in the act of
    - του (μη) + infinitive: for the purpose of (so as not to)
    - The subject goes in the accusative: δια το τον στρατηγον ἀπειναι (because the general was away).
- Also used in indirect statement and command, after ὡστε (result), and after πριν (before).
- The negative is μη, except in indirect statement.

### Uses of the participle

- Attributive, with the article: οἱ φευγοντες (those who are fleeing), ὁ παρων καιρος (the present moment). ὁ βουλομενος means ‘anyone who wants’.
- Circumstantial, describing the circumstances of the main verb: time, cause, condition, concession or means. Context decides: ταυτα ποιησας ἀπηλθε (having done this, he left; after doing this, he left).
    - ὡς + participle gives an alleged reason (‘on the grounds that’); ἁτε + participle a real one (‘since’).
    - καιπερ + participle: although (see Concessive).
    - The future participle, often with ὡς, shows purpose: ἠλθε λυσομενος την θυγατερα (he came to ransom his daughter).
- Genitive absolute: a noun or pronoun and a participle in the genitive, not referring to anything in the main clause: του στρατηγου κελευσαντος, οἱ στρατιωται ἀπηλθον (when the general gave the order, the soldiers left). Translate flexibly: when, since, although, after.
- Accusative absolute: impersonal verbs use the neuter accusative instead: ἐξον ἀπιεναι, ἐμεινε (although it was possible to leave, he stayed).
- Supplementary, completing certain verbs:
    - τυγχανω + participle: happen to: ἐτυχε παρων (he happened to be present)
    - λανθανω + participle: escape notice, do secretly: ἐλαθε φυγων (he escaped unnoticed)
    - φθανω + participle: do first, get ahead of: ἐφθησαν ἀφικομενοι (they arrived first)
    - διατελεω + participle: keep on doing
    - ἀρχομαι, παυομαι + participle: begin, stop doing
    - χαιρω + participle: enjoy doing
    - φαινομαι + participle: is clearly; + infinitive: seems: φαινεται σοφος ὠν (he is clearly wise), φαινεται σοφος εἰναι (he seems to be wise)
- After verbs of knowing and perceiving, a participle gives an indirect statement (see Indirect speech).
- The negative is οὐ for facts, μη for conditions and generalisations.

## Comparison & verbal adjectives

> Genitive of comparison, verbal nouns, verbal adjectives in -τεος

### Comparison

- See Adjectives & adverbs for forming comparatives and superlatives.
- ἠ + the same case, or the genitive of comparison: σοφωτερος ἐστιν ἠ ὁ ἀδελφος, or σοφωτερος ἐστι του ἀδελφου (he is wiser than his brother).
- Dative of measure of difference: πολλῳ σοφωτερος (much wiser), ὀλιγῳ (a little).
- ὡς or ὁτι + superlative: as… as possible: ὡς ταχιστα (as quickly as possible).
- Comparative + ἠ ὡστε + infinitive: too… to…: μειζων ἐστιν ἠ ὡστε φερειν (it is too big to carry).
- For ‘as… as’, use the correlatives: τοσουτος… ὁσος (as big as), τοιουτος… οἱος (of the same kind as).

### Verbal nouns

- Greek has no gerund. The articular infinitive (το + infinitive) does the job (see Uses of the infinitive).

### Verbal adjectives

- In -τεος, -τεα, -τεον: obligation (‘must be done’), like the Latin gerundive. Formed from the verb stem, often the aorist passive stem with τ for θ (ἐπραχθην → πρακτεος).
- The agent goes in the dative.
- Personal: agreeing with a noun: ἡ πολις ἡμιν ὠφελητεα ἐστιν (the city must be helped by us).
- Impersonal: neuter -τεον (or -τεα), often with an object: ἡμιν πολεμητεον ἐστιν (we must go to war); ποιητεον ἐστι ταυτα (these things must be done).
- In -τος: possibility or a passive sense: γνωστος (known, knowable), ὁρατος (visible).

## Uses of ἀν

> Potential, unreal, iterative, indefinite

- ἀν has no single translation. It marks a verb as potential, unreal or indefinite.
- With the optative: potential (‘would, could, might’): ἡδεως ἀν ἀκουσαιμι (I would gladly hear).
- With a past indicative: unreal (‘would be’, ‘would have’): imperfect for the present, aorist for the past: ἐνικησαν ἀν (they would have won). This is the apodosis of an unreal conditional.
- With the imperfect or aorist indicative, it can also mean ‘used to’ (iterative): ἐλεγεν ἀν (he would say, he used to say).
- With the subjunctive in indefinite clauses, joined onto the conjunction: ἐαν (εἰ + ἀν), ὁταν (ὁτε + ἀν), ἐπειδαν (ἐπειδη + ἀν), ὃς ἀν (whoever).
- In indirect speech, ἀν stays with the infinitive or participle if it was in the original: οἰομαι αὐτον ἐλθειν ἀν (I think he would come).
- ἀν never comes first in its clause; it usually follows the verb, a negative, or an emphatic word.
- At the start of a clause, ἀν can be a contraction of ἐαν (if).

## Indirect speech

> Statement, question, command, and subordinate clauses within

### Indirect statement

There are three constructions, depending on the introducing verb.

- ὁτι or ὡς + a finite verb (after λεγω, ἀγγελλω and others): λεγει ὁτι ὁ στρατηγος παρεστιν (he says that the general is here).
    - Greek keeps the tense of the original; English shifts it back.
    - In historic sequence, the verb may change to the optative of the same tense, or stay as it was: εἰπεν ὁτι ὁ στρατηγος παρειη (he said that the general was here).
- Accusative + infinitive (after φημι, νομιζω, οἰομαι, ἡγεομαι and often λεγω): νομιζω τους πολεμιους ἀπελθειν (I think that the enemy have left).
    - The infinitive keeps the tense of the original: present for the same time, aorist for before, future for after.
    - If the subject is the same as the introducing verb’s, it is left out, and any words agreeing with it go in the nominative: ἐφη σοφος εἰναι (he said that he was wise).
    - The negative is οὐ (οὐ φημι, I deny).
- Participle (after verbs of knowing and perceiving: οἶδα, γιγνωσκω, ὁραω, ἀκουω, αἰσθανομαι, μανθανω, πυνθανομαι, and ἀγγελλω): ὁρω σε ψευδομενον (I see that you are lying). With the same subject, the participle goes in the nominative: οἶδα ἁμαρτων (I know that I made a mistake).
- ἀν is kept wherever it was in the original.
- Extended reported speech: in long reports (common in Xenophon), main verbs carry on in the infinitive, or the optative after ὁτι, even across several sentences. Subordinate clauses follow the rules below.

### Indirect questions

- Introduced by a question word, often in its indirect form (ὁστις, ὁπου, ὁπως, ὁποτε, ὁσος), by εἰ (whether), or by ποτερον… ἠ (whether… or).
- The mood of the direct question is kept. In historic sequence it may change to the optative: ἠρετο ὁποι πορευοιντο (he asked where they were going).

### Indirect commands and prohibitions

- Infinitive after verbs of ordering, asking and advising (κελευω, παρακαλεω, πειθω, ἀξιοω): ἐκελευσε τους στρατιωτας μενειν (he ordered the soldiers to stay). The negative is μη: ἐκελευσε μη μενειν.
- κελευω takes the accusative of the person; δεομαι (ask) the genitive; παραινεω (advise) the dative.
- Effort clauses (‘see to it that’): ὁπως + future indicative, after verbs of planning, trying and taking care (σκοπεω, ἐπιμελεομαι, πραττω): ἐπεμελειτο ὁπως οἱ στρατιωται σιτον ἑξουσιν (he took care that the soldiers would have food). The negative is ὁπως μη.

### Subordinate clauses in indirect speech

- Primary sequence: the mood of the original is kept.
- Historic sequence: a subordinate verb may stay as it was, or change to the optative of the same tense:
    - an indicative in a primary tense → optative
    - a subjunctive with ἀν → optative without ἀν (ἐαν → εἰ, ὁταν → ὁτε, ἐπειδαν → ἐπειδη, ὃς ἀν → ὃς)
    - past indicatives (imperfect, aorist) usually stay unchanged.
- e.g. ἐαν ἐλθῃς, ἀπειμι (if you come, I shall leave) → εἰπεν ὁτι, εἰ ἐλθοι, ἀπιοι (he said that if he came, he would leave).
- Pronouns and words like ‘here’ and ‘now’ shift as in English: ἐγω → αὐτος or ἐκεινος, ἐνθαδε → ἐκει, νυν → τοτε.

## Subordinate clauses

> Description, purpose, result, conditional, causal, temporal, indefinite, fearing, concessive, comparative

### Description: relative clauses

- ὅς, ἥ, ὅ (or ὁστις) + indicative; the negative is οὐ.
- The relative agrees with its antecedent in gender and number; its case comes from its job in the relative clause.
- Attraction: the relative often takes the case of a genitive or dative antecedent: ἐκ των πολεων ὧν ἐχει (from the cities which he has; ὧν instead of ἁς).
- The antecedent is often left out: ὧν ἐχει (of the things which he has), οἱ (those who).
- Relative + future indicative can express purpose: πεμπουσι πρεσβεις οἱ ἐρουσιν (they send envoys to say).

### Description: participial constructions

- An attributive participle with the article does the work of a relative clause: οἱ ἐν τῃ πολει μενοντες (those who were staying in the city).
- With μη, it means ‘anyone who’: οἱ μη πιστευοντες (anyone who doesn’t believe).

### Purpose

- ἱνα, ὁπως or ὡς + subjunctive in primary sequence; optative (or subjunctive) in historic: ἠλθεν ἱνα ἰδοι (he came to see). The negative is μη (ἱνα μη, or just μη).
- Future participle, often with ὡς: ἠλθεν ὡς ὀψομενος (he came to see).
- Relative + future indicative: πεμπουσι πρεσβεις οἱ ἐρουσιν (they send envoys to say).

### Result

- ὡστε (so that, so as to), often after a signpost word: οὑτως (so), τοσουτος (so great), τοιουτος (such).
- ὡστε + indicative: an actual result, which did happen. The negative is οὐ: οὑτως ἐφοβουντο ὡστε ἐφυγον (they were so afraid that they fled).
- ὡστε + infinitive: a natural or likely result. The negative is μη: οὑτως ἐφοβουντο ὡστε φευγειν (they were so afraid as to flee). A different subject goes in the accusative.
- At the start of a sentence, ὡστε can just mean ‘therefore’.

### Conditional

- The protasis (the ‘if’ clause) takes μη; the apodosis takes οὐ.

| Type | Protasis | Apodosis |
|---|---|---|
| Open (present or past) | εἰ + indicative | indicative |
| Future more vivid | ἐαν + subjunctive | future indicative |
| Future less vivid | εἰ + optative | optative + ἀν |
| Unreal present | εἰ + imperfect | imperfect + ἀν |
| Unreal past | εἰ + aorist | aorist + ἀν |
| General present | ἐαν + subjunctive | present indicative |
| General past | εἰ + optative | imperfect indicative |

- Open: εἰ τουτο λεγει, ἁμαρτανει (if he says this, he is wrong)
- Future more vivid: ἐαν τουτο λεγῃ, ἁμαρτησεται (if he says this, he will be wrong)
- Future less vivid: εἰ τουτο λεγοι, ἁμαρτανοι ἀν (if he were to say this, he would be wrong)
- Unreal present: εἰ τουτο ἐλεγεν, ἡμαρτανεν ἀν (if he were saying this, he would be wrong)
- Unreal past: εἰ τουτο εἰπεν, ἡμαρτεν ἀν (if he had said this, he would have been wrong)
- General present: ἐαν τις τουτο λεγῃ, ἁμαρτανει (if anyone ever says this, he is wrong)
- General past: εἰ τις τουτο λεγοι, ἡμαρτανεν (if anyone ever said this, he was wrong)
- Mixed conditions are possible: translate each half by its own type.
- εἰ μη means ‘unless’ or ‘if not’; εἰ δε μη means ‘otherwise’.

### Causal

- ὁτι, διοτι, ἐπει or ἐπειδη (because, since) + indicative. The negative is οὐ.
- In historic sequence, a reason given as someone else’s (not the writer’s) can go into the optative.
- Participles: ἁτε + participle (since; a real reason), ὡς + participle (on the grounds that; an alleged reason).
- δια + accusative, or δια το + infinitive (because of).

### Temporal

- ἐπει, ἐπειδη, ὁτε or ὡς (when, after, since) + indicative, for a definite time. The negative is οὐ.
- Greek often uses the aorist where English uses the pluperfect: ἐπει ἀφικοντο (when they had arrived).
- For indefinite time (‘whenever’), see Indefinite clauses.
- ἑως and μεχρι (while, until): indicative for a definite time; ἀν + subjunctive (primary) or optative (historic) for an indefinite one.
- πριν (before, until):
    - After a positive main clause, πριν + infinitive means ‘before’: ἀπηλθε πριν τον στρατηγον ἐλθειν (he left before the general came).
    - After a negative main clause, πριν + indicative (past) or πριν ἀν + subjunctive (future) means ‘until’: οὐκ ἀπηλθε πριν ὁ στρατηγος ἠλθεν (he didn’t leave until the general came).
- ἐν ᾧ (while), ἐξ οὗ (since, from the time when).

### Indefinite

- ‘Whoever, whatever, whenever, wherever, if ever’: relative, temporal or conditional clauses about no particular person or time.
- Primary sequence: ἀν + subjunctive: ὃς ἀν τουτο ποιῃ (whoever does this), ὁταν ἐλθῃ (whenever he comes), ἐαν (if ever).
- Historic sequence: optative without ἀν: ὃς τουτο ποιοι (whoever did this), ὁτε ἐλθοι (whenever he came).
- The negative is μη.
- The main verb is usually present or future in primary sequence, and imperfect in historic (for a habit): ὁτε ἐλθοι, ἐχαιρομεν (whenever he came, we were happy).

### Fearing, prevention and precaution

- Fearing (φοβεομαι, δεδοικα, δεδια) + μη + subjunctive in primary sequence; optative (or subjunctive) in historic: φοβουμαι μη ἐλθῃ (I’m afraid that he may come).
- The negative is μη οὐ: φοβουμαι μη οὐκ ἐλθῃ (I’m afraid that he may not come).
- A fear about the present or past takes μη + indicative: φοβουμαι μη ἡμαρτηκαμεν (I’m afraid that we have made a mistake).
- Afraid to do something: + infinitive: φοβουμαι μενειν (I’m afraid to stay).
- Precaution (taking care that something doesn’t happen): ὁπως μη + future indicative, or φυλαττομαι / ὁραω μη + subjunctive: ὁρα μη ἀπατηθῃς (take care that you aren’t deceived).
- Prevention: κωλυω + infinitive: ἐκωλυσε αὐτους ἀπελθειν (he prevented them from leaving).
    - Verbs of hindering and denying often add a redundant μη, which isn’t translated: εἰργει αὐτους μη ἀπελθειν (he prevents them from leaving).
    - If the main verb is negated, this becomes μη οὐ: οὐκ εἰργει αὐτους μη οὐκ ἀπελθειν (he doesn’t prevent them from leaving).

### Concessive

- καιπερ + participle (although): καιπερ πολλα παθων, οὐκ ἐπαυσατο (although he suffered a lot, he didn’t stop).
- και εἰ, καν (= και ἐαν) (even if) and εἰ και (although) + the usual conditional construction.
- καιτοι (and yet) and μεντοι (however) as connectives.
- A genitive absolute or other circumstantial participle can be concessive from context.

### Comparative

- ὡς or ὡσπερ (as, just as) + indicative: ἐποιησε ὡσπερ ἐκελευσας (he did as you ordered).
- ὡσπερ + participle: as if: ἐσιγα ὡσπερ οὐκ ἀκουων (he was silent as if he didn’t hear).
- Correlatives: τοσουτος… ὁσος (as much as), τοιουτος… οἱος (such as), οὑτως… ὡς (so… as): τοιουτος ἐστιν οἱος ὁ πατηρ (he is just like his father).
- ὁ αὐτος + dative: the same as.

## Metre

> Iambic trimeter, long and short syllables

- Unseen verse for A Level is tragedy (Euripides or Sophocles) in iambic trimeter, and you have to scan two lines.
- To check your scansion, compare against a commentary on the play.

### Iambic trimeter

- Three metra of x – ⏑ –, where x (anceps) can be long or short:

x – ⏑ – | x – ⏑ – | x – ⏑ –

- Traditionally counted as six feet, each an iamb (⏑ –). Feet 1, 3 and 5 can be a spondee (– –) instead.
- Resolution: a long syllable can be replaced by two shorts. This gives a tribrach (⏑ ⏑ ⏑) in any foot except the 6th, a dactyl (– ⏑ ⏑) in feet 1 and 3, and an anapaest (⏑ ⏑ –) in the 1st foot (or anywhere for a proper name).
- The 6th foot is always an iamb, but its last syllable can be short.
- The caesura usually falls after the 5th syllable, or sometimes after the 7th.
- Syllables 3, 7 and 11 (the short of each metron) are almost always short; this is the best anchor when scanning.

### To scan a line

1. Mark elisions (usually shown with an apostrophe: δ’, ἀλλ’) and crasis (καν = και ἀν, τἀμα = τα ἐμα). Crasis counts as one syllable.
2. Count the syllables. Twelve means no resolution; more means resolution somewhere.
3. Mark the syllables that are long by nature: η, ω, diphthongs, any vowel with an iota subscript, and any vowel with a circumflex.
4. Mark the syllables that are short by nature: ε and ο.
5. Mark syllables long by position: a vowel before two consonants or a double consonant (ζ, ξ, ψ), even across a word break.
6. α, ι and υ can be either; let the pattern decide.

### Notes

- Mute + liquid: before a mute (π, β, φ, κ, γ, χ, τ, δ, θ) followed by a liquid (λ, ρ, μ, ν), a short vowel usually stays short in drama (πατρος can start ⏑ ⏑).
- Synizesis: two vowels in a word sometimes merge into one long syllable (θεος as one syllable; -εως in πολεως).
- Unlike Latin, ε and ο are always short and η and ω always long, so many syllables can be marked straight away.

## Unseen & prose tips

> Personal tips for translation and composition

### Unseen translation

- Use the English! If there’s a few lines of English for context, use it for vocab and for a general idea of what’s going on. In verse, generally there’ll be around three major points.
- If word order is throwing you off, go very literally. Translating each word by itself can give you a general idea of what’s going on.
- If in doubt about how to translate something, stay literal.
- Sometimes reading ahead to the next sentence gives enough context to fill in the blanks.
- For words you don’t know, try to derive a meaning from a cognate or from context.
- Don’t jump ahead in guessing what’s going on; it can lead you down a slippery slope and waste time.
- In verse, imperatives are much more common, so be careful not to mix them up with nouns.
- Split sentences (or lines) at hard punctuation, then into clauses where possible.
- Marking in agreements beforehand never hurts.
- In Xenophon, sentences often stack participles and genitive absolutes before the main verb. Find the main verb first.
- In verse, scan the lines to tell apart forms that look ambiguous.

### Particles

Most come second in their clause, and many are best left untranslated or rendered by tone.

| Particle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| μεν… δε | on the one hand… on the other; often just ‘and’ or ‘but’ |
| δε | and, but |
| γαρ | for, because |
| οὖν | so, therefore |
| δη | indeed, then (adds emphasis) |
| γε | at least, at any rate |
| τε… και, και… και | both… and |
| ἀλλα | but |
| μεντοι | however |
| καιτοι | and yet |
| ἀρα | then, so (not the question ἆρα) |
| που | I suppose, surely |

### Prose composition

- Use any context where possible.
- If you’re unsure how to translate a phrase or word, find a synonymous phrase or word to replace it.
- Watch the agreements carefully.
- Write breathings: OCR expects them in composition. Accents aren’t needed.
- Don’t rush into chasing style marks; it’s better to be literally correct than to make a mistake going for an extra mark.
- Style marks can come from (not exhaustive):
    - connecting every sentence to the last (δε, γαρ, οὖν, και); Greek rarely leaves a sentence unconnected
    - correct word order, especially particles that come second (δε, γαρ, οὖν, μεν)
    - μεν… δε for contrasts
    - participles and genitive absolutes instead of subordinate clauses
    - the article sandwich (ὁ ἐν τῃ πολει ἀνηρ, the man in the city)
